When work is happening across multiple sites, the owner often knows that teams are busy but cannot quickly reconstruct the actual financial position. Effective project control begins by connecting the work performed to the commitments and evidence behind the numbers.

Activity is not the same as progress

A site can look productive while costs are drifting. Material has been purchased, subcontractors are working, and photographs show activity. But which milestones are complete? What has been billed? Which deductions or retentions affect collection? What cost has been incurred but not yet recorded?

For contractors, operational visibility and financial visibility need to be close enough to support decisions. They do not have to be one huge software module, but they cannot rely entirely on unrelated spreadsheets.

Track these six dimensions of a project

1. Contract scope and baseline

Record the agreed work scope, important quantities, commercial milestones and key assumptions. If scope changes, preserve the original baseline and make the revised context visible. Otherwise budget comparisons become misleading.

2. Site progress

Use consistent site updates that distinguish work completed from work merely started. For high-impact milestones, connect photographs, dates and the responsible team member. Avoid turning daily reporting into a photo dump without interpretation.

3. Materials and commitments

Material purchased for a project is not always material consumed on it. Similarly, a supplier commitment is not necessarily a paid expense. A useful operating picture distinguishes ordered, received, issued and outstanding amounts where the process requires it.

4. Subcontractor exposure

Track assigned scope, completed quantities or milestones, certified bills, advances, retentions and disputes as applicable. Overlooking subcontractor commitments can make a job seem more profitable than it really is.

5. Client billing

Running-account billing and certificates may include retention, recoveries, advances or other agreed deductions. Distinguish gross billed value from certified, collectible and received amounts.

6. Cash and receivables

A profitable contract can still put pressure on cash flow. Keep a clear list of certified amounts, unpaid balances, expected payment milestones and blockers that require customer follow-up.

Scenario: a project appears 70% completePhysical progress does not mean 70% of profit has been earned or collected. Some material may have been purchased early, subcontractor bills may be pending, and client certificates may carry deductions. Review progress, committed cost and billing independently before concluding the project is healthy.

Questions for the weekly project review

  • What changed on site since the previous report?
  • Which activities are behind schedule, and why?
  • What costs were incurred, committed or approved this week?
  • Which supplier or subcontractor decisions need attention?
  • What is billed, certified, still receivable, or disputed?
  • Which important document or site evidence is missing?

Common reporting traps

Mixing estimates with actual expenditure: label forecasts and recorded costs differently. Only tracking paid bills: unpaid commitments can be material. Confusing receipts and revenue: money received is not necessarily the same as work earned. Ignoring deductions: the expected cash amount may differ substantially from the gross certificate.

How to choose a project dashboard

Ask for a realistic walkthrough of one project: expense submission, review, BOQ context, running bill, deduction and receivable. The system should make the key relationship clear while maintaining appropriate permissions and evidence. Do not assume a feature shown in a development preview has already been fully accepted for production.

The goal is simple: when an owner asks 'How is this project really doing?', the answer should rely on connected records rather than a long round of phone calls.
