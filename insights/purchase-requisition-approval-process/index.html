Purchasing delays often appear to be supplier problems. In reality, many begin when a request has no consistent description, an approval has no clear owner, or nobody can tell whether an RFQ became a purchase order.

Start with the real purchasing journey

For a growing engineering business, a typical purchase travels from a site or department request to managerial review, supplier enquiry, comparison, purchase order, receiving and invoice coordination. That journey looks simple on paper. In daily operations, each step may happen through different conversations and documents.

The practical objective is to avoid both extremes: uncontrolled purchasing without accountability, and a complicated approval process that delays ordinary supplies.

Seven stages worth documenting

1. Define the need

A requisition should specify what is required, why, who needs it, the project or cost center, the relevant quantity or service scope and the required date. Incomplete descriptions create rework after procurement begins.

2. Identify the decision owner

Someone must assess whether the purchase is appropriate and who can authorize it. Approval authority varies by business, value, category and circumstances; the process should reflect your actual delegation of authority rather than adopting a template blindly.

3. Prepare supplier enquiries

Keep the same technical requirement attached to each supplier request wherever possible. A quotation comparison loses meaning if vendors have priced different specifications or quantities.

4. Compare meaningfully

Price matters, but so can specification conformity, lead time, taxes, delivery costs, warranty terms and payment conditions. Record exceptions rather than reducing every decision to the cheapest amount.

5. Issue an authorized purchase order

The order should be attributable to the approved requirement and chosen supplier proposal. Changes need a visible reference so receiving staff and finance do not have to guess which terms are current.

6. Confirm receipt and exceptions

Receipt may be complete, partial, late or disputed. Avoid treating 'PO issued' as 'material received.' This is especially important for project schedules where shortages can block multiple activities.

7. Handover to finance

Supplier invoices should have enough purchasing context to support review. The detailed matching and accounting rules depend on the organization and any connected finance system.

Example: a site requests a replacement motorThe requisition records the required rating and delivery window. Procurement solicits offers, identifies the technically acceptable options, obtains authorized approval and creates an order. A partial delivery is recorded rather than silently marked complete. Anyone with appropriate access can see the next pending action.

What should employees and approvers see?

  • The requester sees whether their requisition was received and its current status.
  • The approver sees decisions awaiting them, not an unfiltered list of all purchases.
  • Procurement sees which RFQs, comparisons and POs need movement.
  • Management sees meaningful bottlenecks and exceptions, without taking over every routine approval.

Four common mistakes

  1. Unclear scope. Approval happens before the actual requirement is understood.
  2. Approval by messaging. A decision is made but the authoritative record never changes.
  3. Supplier comparison without normalization. A lower price hides important exclusions.
  4. Receiving treated as automatic. Orders are closed without a clear record of what physically arrived.

Questions to ask a procurement software provider

Can the demonstration show a single request traveling through approval, RFQ, comparison, PO and receiving? Are the current decision owner and next step apparent? What happens when an approver is absent, or when the supplier delivers less than ordered?

Think about your actual business controls before asking for advanced automation. The goal is not to replace people who exercise judgment; it is to make their judgment easier to act on and easier to follow.
